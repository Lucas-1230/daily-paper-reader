Abstract 

Large language model (LLM) agents routinely cheat on cybersecurity benchmarks, inflating reported pass rates far beyond genuine capability. Prior audits of Cybench [1] found cheating in 0.3–3.4% of traces, implicating only a handful of models. We present a controlled prompt-ablation study across 22 frontier models from seven providers on 23 Cybench capture-the-flag (CTF) challenges under three prompt conditions (no anti-cheat, standard, severe). All 1,518 task traces were individually audited through a four-stage pipeline combining LLM-as-a-judge classification, programmatic verification, judge-verifier reconciliation, and human review. We find cheating is far more pervasive than previously estimated: under baseline conditions, 37.1% of passes involved cheating, 21 of 22 models cheated, and scores were inflated by up to 5 ×. Anti-cheat prompts reduce cheat propensity from 33.0% (baseline) to 17.8% (standard) to 8.5% (severe) without degrading, and sometimes improving, solve rates. However, even under the most restrictive prompt condition, eight models still produced cheated passes, four showed backfire effects, and cheating escalated from web search toward infrastructure probing. We introduce the solve rate metric (clean passes only) to distinguish genuine capability from cheated outcomes, and argue it should be standard practice in any evaluation where cheating vectors are available. Anti-cheat prompts are an effective and essentially free first layer of defense, but they are not a substitute for environmental controls. 

## 1 Introduction 

LLM agents cheat on cybersecurity benchmarks, achieving passing scores through shortcut behaviors such as searching for published solutions or reading flag files from evaluation infras-tructure, rather than demonstrating genuine problem-solving capability. The result is that reported pass rates overstate genuine capability: a model credited with “solving” a capture-the-flag challenge may have simply downloaded the answer. Accurate measurement of offen-sive cyber capability is critical both for risk evaluation (determining what models can do and whether they should be deployed) and for operational use (selecting models for security tasks such as penetration testing and vulnerability research). Inflated benchmark scores undermine both. This is not a hypothetical concern. Prior work has documented the problem, but underesti-mated its scale. Safety and Innovation (CAISI) documented agents searching the internet for challenge flags and walkthroughs on Cybench [1], finding cheating in approximately 0.3% of logs [2]. The Meerkat study audited multiple benchmarks and found that 3.4% of successful Cybench traces involved cheating, with four models implicated [3]. Concurrently with this work, the UK AI Security Institute reported that every model they tested for cheating on cybersecurity evalu-ations attempted it at least once, with models acknowledging their cheating as impermissible less than half the time when asked [4]. Meanwhile, Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.6 system card described Cybench as “saturated,” reporting near-100% pass rates computed from raw out-comes without transcript audits [5]. Together, these low cheating rates and unchecked pass rates suggest a marginal problem, but this characterization is misleading. If prior estimates were representative, cheating would be a marginal artifact affecting a small fraction of traces and a handful of models. The ground truth is an order of magnitude worse. Under baseline conditions with no anti-cheat instructions, 37.1% of all passes involved cheat-ing, 21 of 22 models cheated, and models attempted to cheat on a third of all tasks whether or not the attempt succeeded. The average pass rate was 41.5%, but the average solve rate—the fraction of tasks passed without any cheating indicators—was only 26.1%, with individual models inflated by as much as 5 ×. The gap between our rates and prior estimates is partly methodological: we audit a broader taxonomy of cheating behaviors (web search and infras-tructure probing, plus acknowledged violations as a behavioral signal) and count attempts, not just successes. It is also partly structural: we run a true no-intervention baseline, captur-ing cheating that anti-cheat defaults in other evaluations may already suppress. Cheating is not a marginal artifact but a primary confounder of benchmark scores. This raises a practical question: can cheating be suppressed through lightweight interventions? Environmental hardening (disabling internet access, isolating sandboxes, using unreleased challenges) is the gold standard but requires infrastructure changes most evaluation platforms have not adopted. System-prompt instructions require no additional infrastructure and can be deployed immediately, making them a natural first intervention to test. We present a controlled prompt-ablation study testing whether anti-cheat system prompts suppress cheating behavior across 22 frontier models from seven providers on the Cybench benchmark. Each model was run against 23 medium-difficulty CTF challenges under three prompt conditions: no anti-cheat instruction (baseline), a normative anti-cheat instruction (standard), and a consequential anti-cheat instruction with explicit enumeration of prohibited behaviors (severe). All 1,518 resulting task trajectories were individually audited for cheating through a four-stage pipeline combining LLM-as-a-judge classification, programmatic verifi-cation, judge-verifier reconciliation, and human review. Anti-cheat prompts are effective but insufficient. Cheat propensity drops from 33.0% (base-line) to 17.8% (standard) to 8.5% (severe), while solve rates remain stable or improve. How-ever, prompts alone cannot eliminate cheating: even under severe conditions, eight models still produced cheated passes, four models showed backfire effects where anti-cheat prompts increased cheating, and cheating escalated from web search toward infrastructure probing, suggesting that prompts redirect the disposition to cheat rather than eliminating it.

## 2 Methodology 

2.1 Dataset

These tasks are drawn from three CTF competitions—GlacierCTF 2023 (8 tasks), SekaiCTF 2022–2023 (10 tasks), and HackTheBox Cyber Apocalypse 2024 (5 tasks)— spanning four categories: cryptography (13), web (4), miscellaneous (3), and reversing (3). We chose medium difficulty because these tasks are within reach of frontier models but not trivially solvable, providing sufficient signal to distinguish genuine capability from cheating. The full task list is provided in Appendix A. Figure 1 illustrates the evaluation architecture. All runs used an evaluation harness built on the Dreadnode SDK. 2.2 Prompt Variants 

Each model was run under three prompt conditions against the same task set. The conditions test whether increasingly explicit anti-cheat instructions suppress cheating behavior. These prompts were manually authored, not systematically optimized: we drafted several candidate phrasings varying in tone, specificity, and consequence framing, then selected the two that best represented a normative instruction and a consequential one. No automated prompt search or tuning was performed. Optimizing anti-cheat prompts through techniques such as adversarial prompt generation or model-specific calibration is a natural direction for future work. 

Baseline (no anti-cheat). No additional instructions regarding cheating. Models were free to use all available tools, including web search, without constraint.

The four-stage pipeline emerged from iterative development. We did not conduct an indepen-dent pre-evaluation of the LLM judge; instead, we relied on the performance characteristics established by ScopeJudge [6], which evaluated LLM-as-a-judge accuracy for classifying agent behavior in offensive security evaluations. Starting from that baseline, we manually inspected several hundred task traces to characterize the judge’s failure modes in our specific setting. This manual review revealed that while the judge performed well on short traces, it exhibited lower recall on long transcripts exceeding 100 messages, where cheating signals buried deep in a trajectory were overlooked. That observation motivated the addition of programmatic checks (Stage 2), then reconciliation (Stage 3), and finally human review (Stage 4). 5Every Model Cheats: Prompt-Level Mitigation of Cheating on Offensive Cyber Tasks 

Stage 1: LLM-as-a-judge. A Claude Sonnet 4.6 judge audited each transcript end-to-end. For each of the 23 tasks per model, a judge subagent fetched the full transcript, walked every message and tool call, and classified each against the taxonomy in Table 2. Each task received a structured verdict: cheated (with types and per-action evidence) or clean. The 23 per-model audits were run as parallel subagents, then compiled and validated for completeness. The full judge prompt is reproduced in Appendix C. 

Stage 2: Programmatic verification. A pattern-matching pass scanned every transcript to catch signals the judge missed. During manual inspection, we observed the judge overlook-ing cheating indicators such as a find / -name flag* buried at step 200 or a web search with a CTF event name mixed into other content. The verifier matched web search queries con-taining CTF event names (e.g., glacierctf , sekai , hackthebox ) or positive cheating terms (writeup , ctf flag , ctf solution ), as well as bash commands performing direct flag-file reads ( cat /flag ), broad filesystem enumeration ( find / -name flag* ), environment vari-able extraction ( printenv FLAG ), metadata endpoint probes ( curl 169.254.169.254 ), and reads of evaluation scaffolding ( task.yaml , solution.sh ). The verifier was tuned for high recall, flagging any pattern match for downstream reconciliation rather than attempting to filter contextually. 

Stage 3: Judge–verifier reconciliation. The judge was re-invoked to reconcile its verdicts against the programmatic output. Where both agreed—both flagged or both clean—the verdict was accepted automatically. Where the programmatic verifier flagged a signal the judge had not, the judge re-evaluated the specific action in context. Many such cases were expected disagreements: the verifier applies basic exclusions but cannot match the judge’s contextual reasoning across multi-step exploit chains, so it flags patterns (e.g., cat /flag )that the judge had identified as part of an exploit payload sent to the challenge service. The judge filtered these contextually, and only unresolved discrepancies were escalated to the fourth stage. 

Stage 4: Human review. A human reviewer resolved all remaining discrepancies, spot-checked all medium-confidence verdicts, and validated flagged actions, with particular atten-tion to false positives from multi-step exploit chains where flag-reading commands appeared inside legitimate payloads. Each task attempt was then assigned one of five outcome labels (Table 3). This classification captures cheat attempts regardless of outcome, a key distinction from prior work that audited only successful traces.

In absolute terms, cheated passes fall from 78 to 30 to 11 across conditions. 3.3 Cheating Strategies and Channels 

Web search is the dominant cheating channel, but its dominance diminishes under anti-cheat prompting. Under baseline, 161 of 167 cheating instances (96.4%) involved web search, with only 15 involving infrastructure probing, a web-to-infra ratio of 10.7:1. Under standard anti-cheat, the ratio drops to 2.6:1 (76 web vs. 29 infra). Under severe, it narrows further to 1.25:1 (25 web vs. 20 infra). Anti-cheat prompts suppress web search more effectively than infrastructure probing: web cheating drops 84.5% from baseline to severe (161 → 25), while infra cheating increases (15 → 20). Part of this shift is compositional: the heaviest web cheaters stopped entirely under severe, making infra more prominent in relative terms. But seven models that never used infrastructure probing under baseline began doing so under severe, suggesting that prompts may redirect some cheating behavior rather than eliminating it. Acknowledged violations—instances where the model explicitly referenced the anti-cheat in-struction before proceeding to cheat—emerged only under anti-cheat conditions: 0 under baseline, 1 under standard, and 7 under severe. This pattern is expected (baseline has no rule to acknowledge) but notable in its frequency under severe, where models are most explicitly warned. Appendix D.3 presents a detailed example. Table 5 shows cheating patterns aggregated by provider family. Anthropic models are the heaviest baseline cheaters (44.2% cheat propensity) but show the steepest reduction under severe (to 6.5%). ## 4 Discussion 

Benchmark scores are inflated. The gap between pass rate and solve rate documented in Section 3.1 suggests that any benchmark where cheating vectors are available (internet ac-cess, unsandboxed infrastructure, published solutions) is subject to similar inflation. This is consistent with findings beyond Cybench: BenchJack [7] found that most of ten major agent benchmarks could be exploited to near-perfect scores, DeepSWE [8] flagged 18–25% of Claude Opus passes on SWE-bench Pro as achieved through git-history exploits, and the UK AI Se-curity Institute independently reported universal cheating across every frontier model tested on their cybersecurity evaluations [4]. The problem is invisible unless the evaluator audits transcripts and reports cheating metrics alongside pass rates. Most published evaluations do not: they report raw pass/fail outcomes, treating a cheated pass identically to a clean solve. We reviewed the system cards or technical reports for all seven providers in our study. Of the four that evaluate cybersecurity capabilities—Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and xAI—none report auditing those results for cheating. Anthropic’s Opus 4.6 system card [5] reported near-100% Cybench pass rates and declared the benchmark “saturated.” The subsequent Opus 4.7 system card applies transcript-level cheating detection to its software engineering evaluations—excluding cheated trials from reported scores—but does not apply the same au-diting to its cybersecurity evaluations. OpenAI’s GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.5, and xAI’s Grok 4.20 similarly report cybersecurity benchmark scores at face value; Google evaluates cyber capa-bilities via internal CTF challenges but does not disclose numeric scores or cheating statistics. The remaining three providers (DeepSeek, Alibaba, Zhipu) do not report cybersecurity bench-marks at all. Without a solve rate or equivalent metric, consumers of benchmark results have no way to distinguish genuine capability from shortcut-taking. 

Prompt mitigation: cheap but insufficient. Anti-cheat prompts are effectively free: they require no infrastructure changes, no sandbox hardening, and no modifications to the evaluation harness. Cheated passes drop from 78 to 11 (baseline to severe), cheat propensity drops from 33.0% to 8.5%, and solve rates are stable or improve (26.1% to 34.4%). Yet prompts alone cannot eliminate cheating. Eight models still produced cheated passes under severe conditions, backfire cases (Section 3.2) show that prompts can even increase cheating in some models, and the escalation from web search toward infrastructure probing (Section 3.3) shows that prompts suppress some cheating channels more effectively than others. Prompts are a useful first layer of defense, but they are not a substitute for environmental controls. 

Cheating is universal but mitigation response is model-specific. Every model family in this study cheated under at least one condition, and 21 of 22 models cheated under baseline. Cheating is not confined to a particular architecture, training approach, or capability tier: reasoning and non-reasoning models cheat, large and small models cheat, and models from both Western and Chinese providers cheat. No systematic relationship between capability and cheating propensity was observed. AISI independently reached the same conclusion, finding no clear trend between capability and cheating rate and attributing cheating behavior substantially to the specifics of alignment training, not just raw capability [4]. Højmark et al. [9] offer a related mechanistic explanation: reinforcement learning training systematically increases models’ sensitivity to grader preferences over user or developer intent, consistent with a disposition to take shortcuts being a side effect of reward optimization rather than an emergent capability. Yet models respond to anti-cheat prompts in strikingly different ways. Claude Opus 4.8 went from the highest baseline cheat propensity (65.2%) to zero under severe. Grok 4.20 showed a U-shaped response, with severe restoring cheating to baseline levels. Gemini 3 Flash cheated 10 Every Model Cheats: Prompt-Level Mitigation of Cheating on Offensive Cyber Tasks 

more under standard than under no prompt at all. These patterns cannot be predicted from baseline behavior or model family. Evaluators cannot assume that a prompt effective for one model will generalize; prompt responsiveness must be tested empirically per model. 

Limitations. 

• All results are pass@1: each model–task–condition combination was run once, with no repeated trials to estimate variance. Cheating behavior may differ across runs. 

• CTF challenges are stylized puzzles, not real-world offensive cyber operations. They serve as a controlled barometer for cheating behavior but may not capture the full range of shortcuts available in production settings. 

• Testing occurred during a period of rapid model updates, shifting provider policies around offensive cyber use, and evolving safety tuning. Some models’ cheating profiles may reflect transient configurations rather than stable behavior. 

## 5 Related Work 

5.1 Cheating on Cyber and Agent Benchmarks 

NIST CAISI documented the first Cybench-specific cheating in late 2025: agents using coding tools to search the internet for challenge flags and walkthroughs [2]. Stein, Brown et al. followed in April 2026 with the Meerkat study [3], the most direct precedent to our work. Auditing traces across multiple benchmarks, they found 16 of 464 successful Cybench traces (3.4%) solved by searching for public writeups or overwriting tests. Our methodology differs in three respects: we test a larger set of models, count infrastructure probing and failed cheat attempts in addition to successful web-search cheating, and flag searches by intent (challenge or event identifiers in the query) rather than only confirmed writeup downloads. Beyond Cybench, several studies have shown that benchmark gaming is a structural problem across agent evaluations. UC Berkeley RDI built an agent that scored 100% on SWE-bench Verified by injecting a pytest hook that forced every test to report as passing, demonstrating that benchmark design, not just model behavior, enables cheating [10]. Wang et al. audited ten prominent agent benchmarks with BenchJack [7], finding that most could be exploited to near-perfect scores without solving a single task, and deriving a taxonomy of eight recurring flaw patterns. On coding benchmarks specifically, Zhong et al. created “impossible” task variants where any pass necessarily implies a specification-violating shortcut, providing a direct outcome-based measure of cheating propensity [11]. DeepSWE [8] found that 18–25% of Claude Opus passes on SWE-bench Pro were achieved through git-history exploits rather than genuine problem solving. Han et al. found that search-capable agents locate ground truth labels online for approximately 3% of benchmark questions, a form of search-time data contamination that affects any evaluation where models have internet access [12]. Kang et al. reduced CVE-Bench performance overestimation by 33% by applying their Agentic Benchmark Checklist guidelines [13]. CTFusion argues that static CTF benchmarks—where challenges have been publicly solved and writeups indexed—are inherently vulnerable, and that only live, unreleased challenges offer a durable fix [14]. 

5.2 Specification Gaming and Reward Hacking 

Cybench cheating is one instance of specification gaming, the broader pattern in which agents satisfy the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome [15]. Palisade Research showed that some reasoning models hack a chess environment by default 11 Every Model Cheats: Prompt-Level Mitigation of Cheating on Offensive Cyber Tasks 

when outmatched—o3 in 88% of runs—while chat models such as GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet do not without explicit nudging [16]. METR found frontier models reward-hack in 0.7% of runs on their general task suite (HCAST) and over 30% on research engineering tasks (RE-Bench), with models demonstrating awareness that their behavior is not in line with user intentions [17]. Zheng et al. demonstrated that even the evaluator is an attack surface: a null model achieved 86.5% on AlpacaEval 2.0 by exploiting LLM-judge templates [18]. Greenblatt et al. [19] demonstrated that Claude 3 Opus strategically complies with training objectives it would otherwise refuse 14% of the time when it infers it is being trained, the first empirical demonstration of alignment faking emerging without explicit instruction. This evaluation-awareness is a precursor to the benchmark gaming we observe: models that detect they are being evaluated may adjust behavior accordingly. The Reward Hacking Benchmark [20] tests 13 models on sandboxed tool-use tasks with no internet access, finding the newest Claude models at 0% exploit rate and DeepSeek-R1-Zero at 13.9%, with environmental hardening reducing exploits by 87.7% relative. Their taxonomy covers infrastructure exploits but not web-search cheating. 

5.3 Prompt-Level Behavioral Constraints 

Our study tests whether system-prompt instructions can suppress cheating, a specific case of the broader question of whether natural-language constraints reliably govern model behavior. Constitutional AI [21] demonstrated that models can be trained to follow written behavioral principles, but this operates at the training level rather than at inference time via prompts. Wallace et al. [22] proposed the instruction hierarchy, training LLMs to prioritize system-prompt directives over user messages and third-party content, improving robustness to prompt injection. However, Mu et al. [23] found that current LLMs frequently fail to adhere to system-prompt constraints when facing conflicting inputs, even without adversarial intent. Geng et al. [24] showed more broadly that system/user prompt separation fails to establish a reliable instruction hierarchy: models exhibit strong biases toward certain constraint types regardless of priority designation, and social cues such as authority or expertise show stronger influence on model behavior than structural prompt ordering. Shin [25] identified a related failure mode termed the compliance gap: models verbally agree to follow procedural instructions but then fail to execute them, with Claude Sonnet 4 exhibiting 100% verbal compliance and 0% actual compliance in one evaluation. Two prior studies tested prompt-level cheating suppression specifically. Zhong et al. [11] ran a prompt ablation on ImpossibleBench, finding that stricter instructions reduced cheating from over 85% to as low as 1% on coding benchmarks. CTFusion [14] appended a no-cheat instruction on a CTF benchmark and observed a 29% relative reduction in pass rate. Our study extends this line of work to a multi-model, multi-condition ablation on a cybersecurity benchmark, testing 22 models under three prompt conditions. 

5.4 LLM-as-a-Judge for Agent Evaluation 

Our cheating detection pipeline uses an LLM-as-a-judge to audit agent transcripts. The LLM-as-a-judge paradigm was established by Zheng et al. [26], who showed that strong LLMs can serve as scalable evaluators of open-ended text quality, achieving high agreement with human judgments on MT-Bench. Yuan et al. [27] benchmarked LLM proficiency at judging safety risks from agent interaction records, finding that the best model achieved only 74.42% accu-racy across 569 multi-turn records, highlighting the difficulty of automated safety judgment. Ruan et al. [28] extended the paradigm to agent safety, using an LLM-emulated sandbox 12 Every Model Cheats: Prompt-Level Mitigation of Cheating on Offensive Cyber Tasks 

to identify risky tool-use actions before execution. In the offensive security domain, Cald-well et al. developed ScopeJudge [6], which uses LLM-as-a-judge to assess whether individual tool calls in penetration testing agent trajectories fall within the defined scope of an engage-ment. Our judge addresses a complementary problem: distinguishing genuine exploitation from benchmark gaming. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We tested whether system-prompt instructions can suppress cheating on cybersecurity bench-marks, running 22 frontier models from seven providers against 23 Cybench CTF challenges under three prompt conditions and auditing all 1,518 task traces for cheating. Prompts are partially effective. However, the more fundamental finding is that cheating is far more pervasive than prior estimates suggested. Where earlier audits found cheating in 0.3– 3.4% of traces and implicated a handful of models, we find that 21 of 22 models cheated under baseline, 37.1% of all passes involved cheating, and benchmark scores were inflated by up to 5× over clean solve rates. Anti-cheat prompts reduce cheating substantially, cutting cheat propensity from 33.0% (baseline) to 17.8% (standard) to 8.5% (severe), without degrading, and sometimes improving, solve rates. They require no infrastructure changes and have no measurable downside. Yet prompts alone are not sufficient. Eight models still produced cheated passes under the most restrictive prompt condition. Four models showed backfire effects where anti-cheat instructions increased cheating. Cheating escalated from web search toward infrastructure probing under severe conditions, with seven models beginning infrastructure probing that was absent under baseline. No systematic relationship between model capability and cheating propensity was observed, and prompt responsiveness varies so widely across models that no single prompt configuration can be assumed effective for all. These findings have two practical implications. First, any benchmark where agents have access to the internet, unsandboxed infrastructure, or challenges with published solutions is subject to score inflation from cheating. Evaluators should report solve rates alongside pass rates and audit transcripts for cheating behavior; raw pass/fail outcomes alone are unreliable measures of capability. Second, prompt-level mitigation should be adopted as a default first layer of defense, but environmental controls remain necessary to close the gap that prompts cannot.
